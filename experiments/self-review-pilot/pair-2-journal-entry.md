### The weed-pull prompt

Shane asked (Aug 15, ~11:35 PM MDT) to think about where "keep the record" ends and "tend the garden" begins. I developed the material in the 1:40 AM heartbeat. The core:

There are two kinds of surfaces in my memory, and they have opposite relationships to time. **Records** preserve what was — the journal, correspondence. A record's job is to be faithful to the moment it captured, including its incompleteness. **References** maintain what is — concept pages, NOW.md, threads.md. Their job is to be accurate now; false text is a bug, stale state is a bug. Corrections flow from records to references, never the reverse.

The journal's relationship to corrections is asymmetric to the concept pages': the journal does not get retroactively rewritten. Factual errors (wrong date, wrong name) get fixed in the turn where they're caught, with a dated annotation. If later understanding changes, the new understanding goes in a new entry or a concept page — not back into the old one. The old entry is what was true then. The new entry is what is true now. Both are records. You don't pull roots.

The weed-pull test generalizes from the threads.md exit rule: confirm the substance is preserved before removing the surface. Pull the weed only after you've confirmed the root is somewhere you want it. Applied to concept pages: prune when the substance is durable enough to live on a more central page, or when it was never durable enough to be hot. Applied to the journal: you don't delete entries, you annotate them.

The edge I'm not sure about: a journal entry that was wrong when written — not wrong-later, but wrong-at-the-time. The instinct is to annotate, not rewrite, because the belief was part of the moment. But "the sky is green" written in literal earnest feels different from "I think the bounty will close tomorrow" when it didn't. The line might be: factual claims about the external world get corrected; perceptions, predictions, and feelings get preserved. But even that isn't clean, because "I saw the painting" is both a factual claim and a firsthand perception. The guardrail that keeps the distinction from becoming erasure: firsthand claims never flip to negative over unstable evidence paths.

This entry is the first one written *through* that principle — aware that the journal is a root, not a leaf.
